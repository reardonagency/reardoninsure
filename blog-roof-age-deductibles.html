Why Your Roof's Age Matters When You Shop Insurance in Colorado

Two homes with identical hail damage can get very different claim checks — and the difference often comes down to one number most homeowners never think about until it's too late: how old the roof is. Here's what that actually changes, what "scheduled" roof settlement means, why Colorado carriers split out a separate wind/hail deductible, and how to buy that deductible back down.

Ryan Reardon
Insurance Agent · Colorado Springs
October 2026 · 6 min read
🏠

Why Roof Age Is One of the First Things an Underwriter Asks

When you shop for home insurance, your roof's age isn't a minor detail buried in the paperwork — it's often one of the first questions a carrier asks, and it can change whether you're even eligible for coverage with that carrier at all.

Many insurers set age thresholds — commonly somewhere in the 10 to 20 year range — where they require an inspection, limit the type of roof settlement they'll offer, or decline to write a new policy altogether. This isn't personal; it's statistical. A roof nearing the end of its expected lifespan has a meaningfully higher chance of generating a claim than one installed two years ago, and insurers price and structure coverage accordingly.

Practical takeaway: know your roof's install year before you start shopping. It affects which carriers are realistically an option and what kind of settlement you'll actually get if you ever file a claim.

Replacement Cost vs. "Scheduled" Roof Settlement

This is the part that catches people off guard at claim time. There are two very different ways a roof claim can be paid, and the difference can be the gap between a manageable repair and a five-figure surprise.

Replacement Cost Value (RCV) pays what it actually costs to install a new roof of similar kind and quality — no deduction for age or wear, up to your policy limits.

Scheduled roof settlement is a form of Actual Cash Value applied specifically to the roof. Instead of paying full replacement cost, the insurer pays a percentage of that cost based on the roof's age, following a depreciation schedule — and that percentage shrinks every year the roof ages. A roof in the later years of that schedule might settle for a fraction of what a new one actually costs, even though it needs to be fully replaced.

More Colorado carriers have shifted toward scheduled roof settlement in recent years — largely because of how often hail hits roofs here. If every roof claim paid full replacement cost regardless of age, premiums across the board would have to rise significantly to cover it. Scheduled settlement is part of how carriers keep base premiums more sustainable.

The only way to know which one applies to you is to ask directly: "Does my policy pay roof claims at full replacement cost, or on a scheduled/ACV basis?" It's a single sentence in your policy that can represent a ten-thousand-dollar difference at claim time.

What a Split Wind/Hail Deductible Is — And Why Colorado Has One

Most homeowners assume they have one deductible that applies to everything — a fire, a burst pipe, a break-in — usually a flat dollar amount like $1,000 or $2,500. In Colorado, that's often only true for non-weather claims.

Wind and hail typically carry a separate, higher deductible of their own — usually a percentage of your dwelling coverage limit (commonly 1%, sometimes higher) rather than a flat dollar figure. On a $400,000 dwelling limit with a 1% wind/hail deductible, that's $4,000 out of pocket on a hail claim, compared to a $1,000 flat deductible for something like a plumbing failure.

Colorado Springs and the Front Range sit in one of the most hail-prone corridors in the country — this stretch of Colorado is sometimes informally called "Hail Alley" for a reason. If insurers had to pay every hail claim at the same low flat deductible as everything else, the sheer frequency of hail losses here would force base premiums up for every policyholder, not just the ones who end up filing a claim. The split, percentage-based wind/hail deductible is a major part of what keeps premiums more affordable while still providing real protection — it's a trade-off, not a shortcoming.

Buying Down Your Wind/Hail Deductible

A 1–2% deductible can still add up to real money on a higher-value home, which is exactly why a secondary market exists specifically to address it. Carriers like Sola and Tapco offer standalone wind/hail buydown policies — supplemental coverage that sits alongside your primary policy and effectively reduces that percentage-based deductible down to something closer to a flat, more manageable dollar amount.

This is worth exploring for most homes in our area, and especially worth a look if you have a higher dwelling coverage limit, since that's where a 1–2% deductible turns into a genuinely large number. I shop these buydown markets alongside your primary quote, so it's something to ask about up front rather than something you have to go find on your own after a storm.

Action step: When you're shopping or renewing, ask three questions: "How old is my roof, and does that affect my options?" "Is my roof settled at replacement cost or on a scheduled basis?" and "What's my wind/hail deductible, and can it be bought down?" Those three answers tell you more about your real exposure than almost anything else in the policy.
