Colorado Public Works Bonds: When Contractors Need Payment and Performance Bonds

If you want to bid city, county, school district or state work in Colorado, you will need to be bonded. Here is how the requirement works and how to get ready before the bid is due.

Ryan Reardon
Insurance Agent · Colorado Springs
November 2026 · 6 min read
🏗️

Why Public Projects Require Bonds

On a private job, a subcontractor or supplier who does not get paid can usually file a mechanics’ lien against the property. On public property that is not an option, so state law gives unpaid subs and suppliers a different protection: a payment bond that the general contractor must provide. A performance bond protects the public owner if the contractor cannot finish the work. Colorado’s Public Works Act builds both into the contract process.

The Colorado Thresholds

  • Local governments, over $50,000. Contracts for public works above $50,000 with a county, city, school district or other political subdivision require a payment bond (C.R.S. 38-26-105) and a performance bond (C.R.S. 38-26-106).
  • State of Colorado, over $150,000. The same payment and performance bond requirements apply to state public works contracts above $150,000.
  • Private entities on publicly owned property, over $150,000. These contracts are covered even when private money pays for the work.
  • Smaller jobs. A government may still require a bond on a contract of $50,000 or less if it decides that is in its best interest, so read the bid documents.

The performance bond must be at least half of the contract amount. Always confirm current thresholds in the bid documents or with your attorney, because laws and local rules change.

Federal Projects: The Miller Act

On federal construction contracts above $150,000, the federal Miller Act requires performance and payment bonds. The Miller Act threshold was not raised in the most recent round of federal inflation adjustments, and as of this writing it remains $150,000.

Bid Bonds: The First Step

Before you can be awarded a public contract, you often have to submit a bid bond with your bid. It assures the owner that if you are the low bidder, you will sign the contract and furnish the performance and payment bonds. If you walk away, the owner can claim against the bid bond. The bid documents tell you the required amount, which is often a percentage of your bid. If you do not already have bonding in place, start the process as soon as you decide to bid, because the bid bond is due with the bid.

What Subcontractors and Suppliers Should Know

If you are a sub or supplier on a Colorado public project, the payment bond is your safety net. Colorado law sets a short window to act: suit to recover unpaid amounts must generally be brought within six months after completion of the work. Ask the general contractor for a copy of the payment bond before you start, keep records of what you supplied and when, and talk to a construction attorney quickly if payment stops. How a Contractor Gets Bonded

Reardon Agency places contract bonds through CNA Surety, which offers programs designed for small and growing contractors:

  • Fast Track: credit-based bonding for jobs up to $750,000, with an online application. Contractors in their first year of business ownership can be considered for jobs up to $250,000.
  • Fast Track Pro: bid, performance and payment bonds up to $1,000,000 for a single bond and $1,500,000 in aggregate for qualifying contractors, with improved rates and a streamlined application. It uses company and personal financial statements.
  • Standard underwriting: for larger jobs and project types outside Fast Track, such as design-build, completion bonds, asbestos or hazardous materials work and contracts longer than five years.

A Pre-Bid Checklist

  • Read the bond requirements in the bid documents. Note the bid bond amount, the required bond forms and the deadline.
  • Get prequalified before you need the bond. It is much easier to bid with a bonding line in place than to scramble in the final days.
  • Line up your financials. For larger programs, have your last two years of financial statements and your owners’ personal financial statements ready.
  • Check your insurance. Surety underwriters and project owners also want to see general liability and workers’ compensation in good order.
  • Ask for the bond early. If a job comes with a short fuse, tell your agent the deadline up front.

The Bottom Line

Bonds are not an obstacle to public work, they are the price of admission, and the cost is usually a small percentage of the contract. The contractors who win public jobs tend to be the ones who are prequalified before the bid goes out. If you want to be one of them, start the conversation now.

Quick Answers

When is a payment bond required on a Colorado public works project?

Colorado requires a payment bond on public works contracts above $50,000 with counties, municipalities, school districts and other local governments, and above $150,000 for contracts with the State of Colorado.

How big must a Colorado performance bond be?

The performance bond must be at least one-half of the total contract amount.

Can a new contractor get bonded for public work?

Often yes. CNA Surety’s credit-based Fast Track program considers contractors in their first year of business ownership for jobs up to $250,000, subject to underwriting approval.
